The King’s Gambit starts with **1. e4 e5 2. f4**. White challenges the e5 pawn immediately and offers material to change the central balance.

## An invitation with a cost

If Black accepts with 2...exf4, the e-pawn leaves the center and White’s f-file loses its pawn. That can create opportunities for active pieces later, but moving the f-pawn also exposes the diagonal leading toward White’s king.

The opening therefore puts two questions on the board at once: can White develop quickly enough to use the initiative, and can Black finish development while handling the pressure?

## Activity needs a concrete purpose

A pawn sacrifice does not make every attacking move good. Before bringing another piece forward, check whether your king faces a threat and whether the move creates something the opponent must answer.

White often develops the king’s knight to f3, where it controls h4. That detail matters because a black queen arriving on h4 can otherwise give check along the newly exposed diagonal.

## Try it on the board

Play e4, e5, f4, and exf4. Compare the position before and after White develops Nf3. Notice which squares the knight covers. The lesson is to connect development with a specific defensive or attacking job.
