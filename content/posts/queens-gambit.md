The Queen’s Gambit begins with **1. d4 d5 2. c4**. White asks Black a practical question: keep the pawn on d5, or capture on c4 and change the shape of the center?

## What the pawn offer achieves

After 2...dxc4, Black’s d-pawn has left the central square it occupied. White can consider building a larger center and recovering the pawn through development. The offer is about the position that remains after an exchange, rather than sacrificing material for its own sake.

Black can also decline with 2...e6, reinforcing d5, or choose 2...c6, entering the Slav Defense. These replies create different development problems even though both support the same pawn.

## Resist the urge to resolve everything

A useful way to study this opening is to pause before each central capture. Which file opens? Which pawn becomes a target? Does the exchange help an opposing piece become active?

Do not assume that accepting the gambit wins a pawn permanently, or that declining it means playing passively. Development and the resulting pawn structure matter more than the label.

## Try it on the board

Play d4, d5, and c4. Then compare Black’s e6 and c6 replies. Look specifically at the diagonal available to the bishop on c8. The same central ambition can demand different arrangements of the pieces.
