The Ruy Lopez begins with **1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5**. White develops a bishop while attacking the knight that defends Black’s e5 pawn.

## Pressure travels through pieces

The bishop does not attack e5 directly. It attacks a defender of e5. This gives the opening its useful beginner lesson: a piece can influence a central struggle without pointing at the central square itself.

Black has several ways to respond. The move ...a6 asks the bishop to make a decision, while ...Nf6 develops a knight and adds pressure to White’s e4 pawn.

## The pawn is not automatically free

It is tempting to imagine exchanging on c6 and then collecting e5. Concrete tactics can make that plan fail. The presence of an attacked defender is a reason to calculate, not proof that a pawn can be won.

Likewise, exchanging bishop for knight changes more than one square: it affects the pawn structure and leaves Black with the bishop pair if both bishops remain.

## Try it on the board

Reach Bb5 and inspect the relationships between the bishop, c6 knight, and e5 pawn. Then look at Black’s counterpressure on e4. Studying both players’ threats is more useful than memorizing a sequence from White’s perspective alone.
